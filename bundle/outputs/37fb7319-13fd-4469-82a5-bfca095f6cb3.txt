### Problem 1
**Question:** Let $N = 6,604,416$. How many integers $x$ with $1 \le x \le N$ satisfy $x^{12} \equiv 1 \pmod N$?

**Reasoning:**
First, we find the prime factorization of $N$.
$N = 6,604,416$.
Sum of digits: $6+6+0+4+4+1+6 = 27$, so divisible by 9.
$6,604,416 / 9 = 733,824$.
$733,824 / 9 = 81,536$.
$81,536$ is even. $81,536 / 16 = 5,096$.
$5,096 / 8 = 637$.
$637 = 7 \times 91 = 7 \times 7 \times 13 = 7^2 \times 13$.
So, $N = 9 \times 9 \times 16 \times 8 \times 49 \times 13 = 3^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 2^4 \cdot 2^3 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 13^1 = 2^7 \cdot 3^4 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 13^1$.
Let's re-verify the factorization.
$2^7 = 128$.
$3^4 = 81$.
$7^2 = 49$.
$13^1 = 13$.
$128 \times 81 = 10,368$.
$49 \times 13 = 637$.
$10,368 \times 637 = 6,604,416$. Correct.
So $N = 2^7 \cdot 3^4 \cdot 7^2 \cdot 13^1$.

The number of solutions to $x^{12} \equiv 1 \pmod N$ is the product of the number of solutions modulo each prime power factor, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem.
Let $N_i = p_i^{e_i}$. We need to find $k_i$, the number of solutions to $x^{12} \equiv 1 \pmod{p_i^{e_i}}$.

1.  **Modulo $2^7 = 128$**:
    The group of units $(\mathbb{Z}/2^k\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is isomorphic to $C_2 \times C_{2^{k-2}}$ for $k \ge 3$.
    Here $k=7$, so $(\mathbb{Z}/128\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong C_2 \times C_{32}$.
    The order of the group is $\phi(128) = 64$.
    We want the number of elements $x$ such that $x^{12} \equiv 1$.
    In a cyclic group $C_n$, the equation $x^k=1$ has $\gcd(k, n)$ solutions.
    In a direct product $G_1 \times G_2$, the number of solutions is the product of the number of solutions in each component.
    For $C_2$: $x^{12} = (x^2)^6 = 1^6 = 1$. All 2 elements satisfy this. So 2 solutions.
    For $C_{32}$: We need $y^{12} \equiv 1 \pmod{32}$ in the cyclic group of order 32. The number of solutions is $\gcd(12, 32) = 4$.
    So, $k_1 = 2 \times 4 = 8$.

2.  **Modulo $3^4 = 81$**:
    $(\mathbb{Z}/3^4\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic of order $\phi(81) = 81(1-1/3) = 54$.
    Number of solutions is $\gcd(12, 54)$.
    $54 = 2 \cdot 3^3$, $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$.
    $\gcd(12, 54) = 6$.
    So, $k_2 = 6$.

3.  **Modulo $7^2 = 49$**:
    $(\mathbb{Z}/7^2\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic of order $\phi(49) = 49(1-1/7) = 42$.
    Number of solutions is $\gcd(12, 42)$.
    $42 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$, $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$.
    $\gcd(12, 42) = 6$.
    So, $k_3 = 6$.

4.  **Modulo $13^1 = 13$**:
    $(\mathbb{Z}/13\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic of order $\phi(13) = 12$.
    Number of solutions is $\gcd(12, 12) = 12$.
    So, $k_4 = 12$.

Total number of solutions = $k_1 \times k_2 \times k_3 \times k_4 = 8 \times 6 \times 6 \times 12$.
$8 \times 6 = 48$.
$6 \times 12 = 72$.
$48 \times 72 = 48(70 + 2) = 3360 + 96 = 3456$.

**Answer:** 3456

Let $P_D$ be the property that at least one pair of D's are adjacent.

We want $N(\text{none}) = |S| - |\cup P_i|$.
By PIE:
$N(\text{none}) = \sum (-1)^{|I|} N(I)$, where $N(I)$ is the number of arrangements satisfying the properties in set $I$.

Calculating $N(I)$ involves "gluing" identical letters together.
If we glue $k$ identical items into a single block, we reduce the count of that item by $k-1$ and treat the block as a new distinct item. However, since the items are indistinguishable, we must be careful. A standard way to handle "at least one adjacent pair" in PIE for multiset permutations is to define the "bad" events as specific adjacencies, but that gets complicated with multiple identical items.
A more robust method for "no two identical adjacent" is to use the formula involving sums over partitions or generating functions, but PIE on "blocks" is standard.
Let's define the "bad" events more carefully.
Actually, it is easier to calculate the number of valid arrangements directly using the method of "placing separators" or recursive counting, but with 4 types, it's complex.

### Problem 5
**Question:** How many triples (a, b, c) of positive integers with a < b < c satisfy lcm(a, b, c) = 10,080 and gcd(a, b, c) = 1?

**Reasoning:**

### Problem 6
**Question:** A path from (0, 0) to (12, 8) is made of unit steps, each either right (x goes up by 1) or up (y goes up by 1). How many such paths never visit a point with y > x, and never visit the point (5, 3)?

**Reasoning:**
Total paths from (0,0) to (12,8) staying below $y=x$ (i.e., $y \le x$).
This is a Ballot Problem / Catalan-like number.
Number of paths from $(0,0)$ to $(n,m)$ with $n \ge m$ staying $y \le x$ is $\frac{n-m+1}{n+1} \binom{n+m}{m}$.
Here $n=12, m=8$.
Total valid paths = $\frac{12-8+1}{12+1} \binom{20}{8} = \frac{5}{13} \binom{20}{8}$.
$\binom{20}{8} = 125,970$.
$5/13 \times 125,970 = 5 \times 9,690 = 48,450$.

Now subtract paths that visit $(5,3)$.
$(5,3)$ satisfies $3 \le 5$, so it is in the valid region.
Paths through $(5,3)$ = (Paths $0,0 \to 5,3$ staying $y \le x$) $\times$ (Paths $5,3 \to 12,8$ staying $y \le x$).
Part 1: $0,0 \to 5,3$. $n=5, m=3$.
Count = $\frac{5-3+1}{5+1} \binom{8}{3} = \frac{3}{6} \binom{8}{3} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 56 = 28$.
Part 2: $5,3 \to 12,8$. Shift to $(0,0) \to (7,5)$.
$n=7, m=5$.
Count = $\frac{7-5+1}{7+1} \binom{12}{5} = \frac{3}{8} \binom{12}{5}$.
$\binom{12}{5} = 792$.
$3/8 \cdot 792 = 3 \cdot 99 = 297$.
Total bad paths = $28 \times 297 = 8,316$.

Valid paths = $48,450 - 8,316 = 40,134$.

**Answer:** 40134
